FOREWORD

Ganita-yukti-bhdasda edited in its Malayalam, Sanskrit and English versions, is
a highly erudite and elaborate treatise setting out the rationale of numerous aspects
of Indian Mathematics and Astronomy. An extensive treatise, originally composed
in the medieval Malayalam language, in about 1530 A.D., the work has had a
later Sanskrit translation, may be, a couple of centuries later. The English
translation has been prepared by the present Editor, Prof. K. V. Sarma, for the
benefit of the modem student of Indian mathematics and astronomy.

The author of the Malayalam original, Jyesthadeva (1500-1610 A.D.), a
Namputiri Brahmin of Central Kerala, was a highly erudite scholar, belonging to a
long lineage of astronomers of Kerala who have enriched the discipline through
several hundreds of works, including original texts, commentaries of earlier texts
and a number of tracts on individual topics on the subject, both in Sanskrit and in
Malayalam. Ganita-yukti-bhdsa, the work edited here, tops all the other texts on
rationale, in matters of fullness, extent and elaborateness in exposition. Perhaps,
there has been no other text in astronomical literature produced in India or
elsewhere which is as full and elucidative as Ganita-yukti-bhasa.

Science studies in India are as old as Indian civilization itself, which is one of
the earliest of world civilizations. This would be evident from an incisive perusal
of Vedic literature right from the Rgveda which is dated earlier than 3000 B.C. and
which itself forms only a collection (samhita) of religious and sacerdotal hymns
culled out and specially arranged from the then floating saga of such hymns.
These hymns indeed reflect a highly evolved culture, practically in all fields of
human life. It is interesting that this literature exhibits even scientific insights
and sets out scientific applications in profusion and that too in such a casual
manner as if they were commonly known in the society. The use of decimal
numeration (Rgveda 11.25.18; VI.48.15; VIII.38.13; Yajurveda XVII.2.2); stitching
and weaving (RV.II.32; IV.9), silk (RV X.85.20), agriculture (RV IV.57), a palace
supported by a thousand pillars (RV. V.62.6); distinction between the perishable
spoken word (vak) and the imperishable visible written language (implied in RV
1.164.39; X.71.4), the medical and surgical feats of the ईशा) gods (RV I.116.14-
16; शा, 1. 12) and many other statements are instances in point.

Specifications are to be found of a number of astronomical phenomena,
such as the genesis of the universe (RV X. 72. 2), configuration of the Sun RV.
1.164.14; 1. 24.8), of the Moon (Aitareya Brahmana, 40.8 and Satapatha Brahmana
