The development of the several disciplines of science in India during the post
vedic, classical and medieval, periods had been phenomenal, when very substantial
literature on these subjects had been produced. For example, A. Seidenberg has
pointed out how the geometry of the Sulba Sitras represents a very high level of
mathematical achievement which precedes the Babylonian and Greek geometries
(Agni, The Vedic Ritual of the Fire Altar, ed. By Fritz Staal, 1983, Berkeley). Naturally
enough, these advances had involved deep thinking, experimentation and the
divining of newer and newer methodologies. But, here too, practically none of
the texts explain, step by step, how the new methodologies had been evolved. This
has been so for two reasons. One, that during the traditional teacher-pupil method
of teaching the underlying principles and step by step derivation of results would
be explained orally by the teacher, and, two, that the texts had all to be composed
in the form of stitras (aphorisms) and karikds (short pithy verses) for ease in
memorization. The commentaries of the texts come in for help to some extent, at
times, but most of the commentaries stop with explaining the text proper and do
not go deeper.

It is here that texts of scientific rationale step in to explain and elucidate the
underlying principles, explain technical terms and set out the relationships between
connected matter. The Ganita-yukti-bhdsa, edited here, as noted earlier, is an
extraordinarily instructive work of this nature. The work is in two Sections,
devoted, respectively, to Mathematics and Astronomy. The presentation of the
rationale deals with all aspects, to wit, definitions, concepts, theories, constants,
computations, demonstration by diagrammatic representation and the like. The
procedure is highly logical, going step by step, first presenting the fundamentals
and gradually building up the argument.
