Thibaut has generally agreed with the notions of Whitney on the origin and development
of the siddh&ntic astronomy. But the following words are to be keenly attended to:

“If we suppose that only a very imperfect knowledge of Greek astronomy was transmitted
fo India, and that Hindu Jyotisas endeavored fo erect on that basis a complete system of
their own, we can understand how there came into existence works of the types of
Sdryasiddhanta_ which, although evincing a fundamental dependence on Greek
astronomy, yet show unmistakable traces of originality in numerous details, remaining
indeed in by far the greatest number of cases inferior to their original, yet hitting here and
there on new devices and methods of undeniable merit and ingenuity. The perfect Hindu
system in that case have to be characterized not either as a mere loan from the Greeks,
nor as a mere adoption in the ordinary sense of the word, but rather as a combination
and further development. And the merit of originality as far as it goes, would belong to the
unknown author of the old Sdryasiddhanta’.

The Siddhantic corpus of astronomy cannot expect a more fair judgment from any scholar
who has really understood it against the background of the Greek and Babylonian
science. These Western scholars had little or almost no material before them to be
considered as indigenous under the overriding impact of the Hipparchian zero point and
theory of epicycles. But still they could feel the existence of something original below
these borrowings in Siryasiddhanta, which as Dikshit has observed had lost its original
form by the time of Varahamihira. It is therefore apparent that if there had been an
original Indian school of astronomy its signature may still be available in the
Stryasiddhanta (at least in some mutilated form) and with appropriate efforts the same
can be deciphered.
